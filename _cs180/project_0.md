When the camera is very close to the face, different facial features (nose, chin, ears) are at noticeably different distances from the lens, even though the differences are only a few centimeters away. Because perspective magnifies near objects far more than far objects, the nose (closest) appears disproportionately large relative to the ears and cheeks (farther away) — this is why close-up selfies exaggerate the nose and make the face look distorted. When you step back, the camera-to-face distance becomes large relative to the small differences in depth across the face. All facial features are almost the same distance from the camera. Their relative sizes stay proportional and the face looks natural. The picture that is taken further represents to how a person usually perceives faces when seeing someone.

The same principle applies to the building in reverse. When you walk close to the building and shoot without zoom with the same building size, the camera is close enough that the close edge of the facade is closer than the far edge. The close corner looks much larger than the farther corner, giveing a stronger sense of depth. It makes the receding side of the building appear more sharply angled/tilted than it really is. This is caused by the exaggeration of the perspective. In comparison, when you zoom in from far away, the long distance from the camera to the facade compared to the short depth differences of the corners of the building makes the facade appears flattened/compressed. Features on the buildings look almost the same size, and the sense of depth is lost.
